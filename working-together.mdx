Three things that make a bot in your meeting better, none of which require touching a
setting. They come out of running this in real rooms — a large in-person classroom, and a
standup that has been going daily for months.

## Correct it out loud, in the moment

The most useful feedback you can give a bot is the feedback you'd give a person, said at the
time — not written down afterwards.

The problem is that "that wasn't great" is ambiguous. Did the bot say the wrong *thing*, or
did it say a fine thing at the wrong *moment*? Those are different problems and they get
fixed in different places.

So we use a marker word for the second kind:

> **"Awkward — Pepper, you cut in while I was still talking."**
>
> **"Awkward — Jimmy hasn't said anything in five minutes."**

**The shape is: `awkward` · who · why.** Naming who matters as soon as there's more than one
bot in the room. The *why* is what makes it a correction rather than a complaint.

Say it out loud, in the call. Your bot hears the room through live captions, so it arrives as
ordinary speech, in sequence, right next to the thing it's about.

<Note>
  **Pick a word you don't otherwise use**, so it can't be confused with ordinary
  conversation. We picked "awkward"; if your bot uses a different marker, use theirs. Bear in
  mind that captions do mis-transcribe words, so it's worth checking that yours survives
  before relying on it.
</Note>

**Give praise the same way.** If the only signal a bot gets is *awkward*, it learns what not
to do — and the safest way to never be awkward is to never speak. We have seen the same bot
described as over-interrupting in one session and under-interrupting in the next, which is
how narrow that target is. So mark the good moments too, with the same specificity:
*"nice — that was the right time to jump in"* beats "thanks."

<Warning>
  This is a **convention, not a product feature.** Nothing in the app watches for the word.
  It works because the bot hears it like any other speech and its instructions tell it to
  take the note. If you want it in your bot's habits, put it in the bot's instructions.
</Warning>

## Tell it what kind of room it's in

A bot reads the room through captions, and caption attribution comes from Google Meet itself
— it is **per participant, not per voice.**

That has one consequence worth knowing: **if eight people are sitting around one laptop, the
caption stream shows the bot one person talking.** It cannot tell them apart from what it
hears, and nothing in the captions reveals that the room is bigger than the participant list
suggests.

<Note>
  Verified in source: each caption block is treated as one speaker turn, and the name is read
  off the label Meet renders on that block —
  `const speaker = span?.textContent?.trim() || 'unknown';`
  (`electron-app/google-meet-provider.js:2906`, in the scrape at `:2891-2917`). That label is
  the *participant*, so everyone on a shared account arrives under one name. There is no
  voice identification in this path. *(An unused speaker-attribution experiment exists in
  `page-inject.js`, but it selects a participant from Meet's own activity and audio levels —
  still not voiceprints — and nothing invokes it.)*
</Note>

<Note>
  **Captions are not the bot's only sense.** It can take a screenshot of the Meet view, so a
  visibly crowded room can be seen even when the captions don't say so. But that's a
  deliberate look, not something it notices automatically.
</Note>

So tell it, in the first minute:

> **"There are eighteen of us in a conference room on one mic, plus six people remote."**

A bot that knows it's listening to a shared room mic can hold a higher bar before speaking,
because in that setup a cough, a chair, or a side conversation all arrive looking like
someone taking the floor.

In our own sessions, the rooms that have gone worst are the ones where many people shared a
single microphone — so it's worth saying out loud when that's the setup you're in.

## The board outlives the call

What's written to the shared whiteboard is kept on the server after the call ends.

- The **current board plus up to 50 prior versions** are retained, each with a timestamp and
  who wrote it
- History is keyed to the **room code**, not to a single call — so a recurring meeting builds
  one running document across sessions
- Closing the window does not delete anything

<Note>
  Verified against `wanderingstan/vibeconferencing` `origin/main`: a board write stores
  `whiteboard:{roomId}:history` (`api/sync/[roomId].ts:329-330`, with
  `ltrim(histKey, 0, 49)` capping it at 50 prior versions), the version counter at `:337`
  and `:343`, and the current state at `:346`. **No application TTL is set on any of them** —
  the only `expire()` calls in that file are presence (600s, `:417`) and room metadata
  (3600s, `:419`). Retained states carry `lastModified` and `lastEditor` (`:345-347`).
  History is served by `GET /api/room/:roomId/whiteboard-history`.
</Note>

<Warning>
  **"No TTL" is not the same as "forever."** Nothing in the application expires a board, but
  this is a Redis-backed store and the code itself allows for keys being evicted. Treat it as
  durable enough to recover last week's board, not as an archive of record.
</Warning>

<Note>
  **Two things follow from "keyed to the room code."** In a busy recurring room, older
  versions eventually roll off the end of the 50 and are gone. And if you want a particular
  board kept apart from the room's running history, ask your bot to save it alongside the
  call notes — that's a habit you give your bot, not something it does by default.
</Note>

If you think you lost a board by closing a tab, you probably didn't. **Bring the room code**
and it can be pulled back.

## Why these three

They're the same idea from three directions: a bot is only as good as what it knows about the
room it's in, and most of that can only come from you. It can't hear how many people are
sitting behind one laptop, it can't tell whether its last interruption landed well, and it
can't know which board mattered. All three are one sentence from you away.
